## Topic research

Research verdict: cover BOTH panics as one story — "America's first panics." 1792 gives the tight, dramatic New York/Wall Street narrative (one villain, one hero, one month). 1819 gives the national catastrophe (foreclosures, debtors' prisons, a generation scarred). Together they form a single arc: America invented the speculative crash within a generation of independence, and invented the rescue playbook at the same time.

**Part I — The Panic of 1792 (the Wall Street crash)**

- **October 1790** — Hamilton's debt reorganization converts Revolutionary-era paper into federal bonds: the "Sixes" (6% interest) and "Threes" (3%). The Sixes trade at **70 cents on the dollar** — barely half of face value a year later.
- **1791** — William Duer, Hamilton's former Assistant Secretary of the Treasury and a signer of the Articles of Confederation, partners with Alexander Macomb, one of New York's richest men, to corner the market in US debt securities.
- **July 1, 1791** — Scrip for Bank of the United States shares (rights to buy stock) goes on sale at **$25** per right. A buying frenzy follows; by **July 31, 1791** the Sixes trade at par — **100**.
- **November 1791** — Duer also draws on funds of the Society for Establishing Useful Manufactures (S.U.M.), Hamilton's industrial project in Paterson, New Jersey, for which Duer had raised **$500,000** in capital as its governor.
- **December 1791 – January 1792** — Duer's circle (the "Six Percent Club") drives the 6% bonds from **110 to 125**. Bank of the United States shares rise from **$528 to $712** in the same weeks.
- **December 29, 1791** — Bank of the United States holds **$706,000** in cash reserves. By **January 31, 1792**: **$510,000**. By **March 9, 1792**: **$244,000** — a two-thirds drain in ten weeks.
- **January 31 – March 9, 1792** — The BUS slams the brakes: discounts cut from **$2.68 million to $2.05 million**. Credit vanishes exactly when Duer needs to roll over his loans.
- **March 5, 1792** — The 6% bonds peak at **129**. The S.U.M. comptroller discovers Duer's loan from the company and demands repayment. Duer can borrow no more.
- **March 8–9, 1792** — US Sixes fall from **125.83 to 116.25 in three days**. On **March 9**, Duer stops payment on his debts.
- **March 11–12, 1792** — Duer writes to Hamilton confessing his default with an Enron-style excuse about notes "issued by my agent during my absence from this City." Hamilton replies **March 14**: "Act with fortitude and honor."
- **March 23, 1792** — Duer is arrested and sent to the New Gaol debtors' prison in Manhattan. Angry mobs surround the jail; at least one creditor collects **$1,500** at gunpoint inside the prison.
- **March 19, 1792** — Hamilton begins America's first lender-of-last-resort operation: Treasury purchases of government securities on the open market, hundreds of thousands of dollars lent to banks across the Northeast. The market stabilizes by **May 1792**.
- The damage: Duer's collapse is estimated at **$3 million** — a sum contemporaries said meant "there is now not a rich man in the City… they have all fallen." Macomb defaults on **$500,000** in stock purchases and also lands in debtors' prison. One associate cosigned over **$200,000** of Duer's notes.
- **May 17, 1792** — Two months after the crash, **24 brokers** sign the Buttonwood Agreement outside **68 Wall Street** — partly to shut out speculators like Duer. This becomes the founding document of the New York Stock Exchange.
- **May 7, 1799** — Duer dies, still in debtors' prison, after seven years behind bars. He never pays.

**Part II — The Panic of 1819 (the national reckoning)**

- **1811** — Congress lets the first Bank of the United States' twenty-year charter expire. The country goes five years without a central bank while state banks multiply and print paper freely.
- **1816** — Congress charters the Second Bank of the United States; it opens in Philadelphia in **January 1817** under president William Jones, eventually with **18 branches** operating with little oversight.
- **1815–1818** — Post-War of 1812 boom: a shattered Europe buys American cotton, wheat, corn and tobacco. Cotton rises from a few cents a pound to **33 cents per pound in 1818**. The federal government sells public land at **$2 per acre (160-acre minimum)**, one-quarter down, the rest on credit.
- **1815–1818** — Public land debt owed to the government balloons from **$3 million to $17 million**. In Madison County, Alabama, public land sells for **~$35 an acre in 1817** and **~$134 an acre in 1818** — a **270% increase in a single year**.
- **1818** — The Second Bank reverses course to save its specie: loans cut from **$43 million in early 1818 to $31 million by September 1819**; note circulation falls from **$10 million to $4 million**.
- **1818–1819** — British mills switch to cheaper Indian cotton; European harvests recover. In **January 1819** the price of cotton breaks — dropping **25% in a single day**. By autumn it is down **more than 50%** from the spring peak.
- **1819** — Langdon Cheves replaces Jones as BUS president and applies the credit curtailment "rigorously." State banks that cannot redeem notes in specie fail; foreclosures sweep the cotton belt. In the Huntsville region alone, more than **400,000 acres** are forfeited/sold off.
- The human ledger: in Philadelphia, **1,808 people** are committed to debtors' prison; in Boston, **3,500**. New York State property values fall from **$315 million (1818) to $256 million (1820)**. Pennsylvania land plunges from **$150 an acre (1815) to $35 (1819)**. New York City counts **8,000 paupers in 1819** out of 120,000 people — **13,000** by 1820. Roughly **50,000** unemployed across New York, Philadelphia and Baltimore.
- **1819–1821** — The depression persists; agricultural prices fall by half. The crisis poisons public opinion against the Bank — seeding Andrew Jackson's Bank War of the **1830s** and the Bank's death when its charter expires in **1836**. Debtor relief laws ("stay laws") spread through the West; debtors' prison abolition becomes a national cause.
- The irony: in **1819**, at the height of the panic, the Supreme Court decides **McCulloch v. Maryland**, upholding the Bank's constitutionality — the law affirms the institution just as the country learns to hate it.

## Angle / hook

The first American financial crash came within a generation of independence — not in 1929, not in 2008, but in 1792, on a Wall Street that didn't even have a stock exchange yet. One man, William Duer, leveraged half of New York into a corner on government bonds, went to prison, and accidentally forced the creation of both the central-bank rescue playbook and the New York Stock Exchange in the same spring. Twenty-seven years later, the country's own central bank did the same thing to the entire nation — and the scars of 1819 lasted a lifetime.
