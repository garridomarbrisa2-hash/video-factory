## Topic research

**The idea before Pullman — Lowell, Massachusetts (1820s)**
- **1822** — Boston investors (Patrick Jackson, Nathan Appleton, Kirk Boott and others) incorporated the **Merrimack Manufacturing Company** and broke ground at Pawtucket Falls on the Merrimack River; first cotton run completed **1823**. The town was named for **Francis Cabot Lowell**, dead since 1817, who had built the first integrated power-loom mill in Waltham in **1814**.
- In **1820** the site was the farming hamlet of East Chelmsford with about **200 people**. Lowell was chartered as a town on **March 1, 1826**, population **2,500**; by **1836**, when it was chartered as a city, it had **18,000**; by **1850, 33,000** — the second-largest city in Massachusetts and America's largest industrial center.
- By 1850 the **5.6-mile canal system** delivered **10,000 horsepower** to ten corporations running **forty mills**, **320,000 spindles**, and roughly **10,000 looms**, producing about **50,000 miles of cloth per year**. Nickname: the **"City of Spindles."**
- The workforce was **80–90% young women** — "mill girls" recruited from New England farm villages starting **1823**. They earned **$3.00–$4.00 per week** (about half male wages); room and board in corporation boarding houses cost **$1.25–$1.50 per week**, deducted from pay.
- Boarding houses held **20–40 women per unit**, **4–6 per room, often 2 per bed**, run by keepers who reported misconduct to mill management. "Habitual absence from worship on the Sabbath" was grounds for dismissal and eviction.
- Mill girls worked **5:00 a.m. to 7:00 p.m., an average of 73 hours per week** (12–14 hours a day, half-day Saturdays); the average workday was about **13 hours**. The workweek only shrank to **60 hours by 1874** under state pressure.
- The women published their own literary magazine, the **Lowell Offering (1840–1845)** — mill workers writing about mill life, funded by the owners. Female operatives struck twice in the **1830s** over wage cuts and campaigned for the ten-hour day in the **1840s**; few strikes succeeded.

**Pullman, Illinois — the model town (1880–1894)**
- **1879** — **George M. Pullman** (1831–1897), president of the **Pullman Palace Car Company** (incorporated **1867**), bought **4,000 acres** 14 miles south of Chicago along the Illinois Central line; **600 acres** for the town itself.
- **April 24, 1880** — groundbreaking. Architect **Solon Spencer Beman** (age 27) designed more than **1,300** brick rowhouses plus the factory; **Nathan F. Barrett** did the landscape. First resident moved in **January 1, 1881**; the **Hotel Florence** (named for Pullman's daughter) was completed the same year.
- Pullman projected rents would return **6% on investment**; they never exceeded **4–4.5%**.
- Population: **3,500 in 1882**; nearly **9,000 by 1885**; about **12,000 residents in 1893** — roughly **6,000 employees** plus dependents.
- Rents averaged **20–25% higher** than comparable housing in Chicago or nearby towns — adjusted even for Pullman's superior sanitation. Workers were **forbidden to buy their homes**; leases could be terminated on a whim; inspectors entered homes for "inspections."
- One church — the **Greenstone Church**, built at a cost cited around **$45,000–$57,000** — was offered for rent at **$300/month** (plus **$65** for the parsonage); no congregation could afford it until a Presbyterian group rented it in **1887**.
- The Arcade building held the shops, theater, bank, post office, and library. The library charged a **$3 annual fee** ($1 for children) — roughly **$100 in today's money** — so few workers used it. Pullman personally vetted theater performances.
- Alcohol was banned town-wide — except at the Hotel Florence bar, where wealthy out-of-town guests drank. Independent newspapers and public meetings were suppressed; the company employed spies.
- **1884** — labor bureaus from 13 states and U.S. Labor Commissioner Carroll D. Wright toured the town; their report was broadly favorable but noted residents had **no voice** in their own government.
- **1889** — Pullman was annexed by the city of Chicago against George Pullman's wishes.

**The Pullman Strike (1894)**
- After the **Panic of 1893**, Pullman cut **more than 2,000 workers** (about a third of the workforce) and slashed remaining wages by **~25%** — but did **not** cut rents, which were **automatically deducted from paychecks**. Some workers reportedly took home pennies after rent.
- A 46-member grievance committee asked to meet George Pullman; he refused and **three committee members were fired on May 10, 1894**. That night, meeting at Turner Hall in nearby Kensington, the workers voted **42–4 to strike**.
- **May 11, 1894** — roughly **3,000–4,000 Pullman workers** walked out. The company closed the plant.
- **June 26, 1894** — **Eugene V. Debs' American Railway Union** (then ~**150,000 members**) launched a national boycott of all trains hauling Pullman cars. Some **125,000–250,000 railroad workers in 27 states** ultimately joined; rail traffic west of Chicago seized up.
- **July 2, 1894** — a federal court issued an injunction against the ARU, justified under the **Sherman Antitrust Act** and federal mail-delivery authority (Attorney General **Richard Olney**, a former railroad lawyer, drove the strategy).
- **July 3, 1894** — President **Grover Cleveland** ordered federal troops into Chicago over the written objection of Illinois Governor **John P. Altgeld**.
- Violence peaked around **July 4–7**: railcars burned, fires set (including at the former World's Columbian Exposition grounds); in Chicago **13 people were killed and 53 seriously wounded** in the July clashes. Strike-wide totals: about **30 strikers killed, 57 wounded** (some accounts place total deaths near **34**), property damage exceeding **$80 million**.
- **July 11, 1894** — Debs and three ARU board members arrested; the ARU office ransacked. The strike collapsed by late July; the ARU was destroyed. Debs served **six months** in the Woodstock, Illinois jail for contempt — and came out a socialist; he later ran for president five times.
- Within days of the strike's collapse, Cleveland signed legislation making **Labor Day** a national holiday (rushed through Congress in **six days**, late June 1894) — widely read as a peace offering to labor.
- **1898** — the **Illinois Supreme Court** ordered the Pullman Company to sell all non-industrial property, including the town's housing. By **1907**, every Pullman residence had been sold into private hands.
- George Pullman died in **1897**; so hated was he by labor that his family buried him at night in a lead-lined coffin under concrete and steel rails, fearing grave desecration. **Robert Todd Lincoln** — Abraham Lincoln's son — later served as Pullman Company president until **1911**.
- **2015** — the town became **Pullman National Monument** (later Pullman National Historical Park), Chicago's first unit of the National Park Service.

**Coal country — scrip and the company store (1880s–1960s)**
- **Company scrip** — private metal tokens or paper issued by coal companies as wages or wage advances, stamped **"non-transferable,"** redeemable only at the **company store**. Companies advancing wages in scrip paid only **50–80 cents on the dollar** — an early payday-loan racket that kept miners permanently in debt.
- A **1913** study in the Political Science Quarterly found black powder sold to miners at an average **62% markup**. The **1922 U.S. Coal Commission** found company-store prices in southern West Virginia's New River district ran **~9% higher** than in Charleston; company stores overall charged **4.2% more** than independents in southern West Virginia and **7% more in Alabama**.
- The **1910 Census of Mines** found many coal operators' mining revenues were *below* their operating costs — they stayed profitable by selling goods and housing back to their own workers.
- In the early **1920s**, the U.S. Coal Commission found **65–80% of miners** in Southern Appalachia and the Rockies lived in company towns (vs. **10–20%** in most of the Midwest, **50%** in Pennsylvania).
- Mine guards, company-paid sheriffs' deputies, and blacklists policed the camps; union organizers were beaten or evicted. This system produced the **West Virginia Mine Wars** — the **Matewan Massacre (May 19, 1920)** and the **Battle of Blair Mountain (August–September 1921)**, the largest armed labor uprising in U.S. history, when around **10,000 armed miners** marched on Logan County and fought a week-long battle until federal troops, including Army bombers, intervened.
- **"Sixteen Tons"** — written by **Merle Travis** (recorded **August 8, 1946**, released 1947 on *Folk Songs of the Hills*) from his family's life in the Muhlenberg County, Kentucky coalfields; the line "another day older and deeper in debt" came from a letter by his brother John. **Tennessee Ernie Ford's 1955** version hit **No. 1** on both country and pop charts and entered the **National Recording Registry in 2014**.
- Scrip was ruled unconstitutional when non-transferable in the early 1900s, and paying wages in scrip effectively ended with the **Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938**, yet scrip lingered in Kentucky and West Virginia until **Congress formally outlawed it in 1967**. The Stearns Company in Kentucky kept issuing scrip until **1964** — in **1939**, only **43.5%** of the **$796,000** in scrip it issued was even spent at its own stores, as Sears Roebuck and Montgomery Ward mail-order catalogs broke the monopoly.

**Gary, Indiana — U.S. Steel's instant city (1906)**
- **1905** — the **United States Steel Corporation** (itself formed **1901** in J.P. Morgan's billion-dollar consolidation) bought **9,000 acres** of dunes and marsh at the south tip of Lake Michigan for a new mill and townsite.
- **1906** — engineers platted the town in spring/summer; construction began that summer; the city was incorporated **July 14, 1906**, named for U.S. Steel's founding chairman **Elbert Henry Gary**. Two subsidiaries ran it: **Indiana Steel Company** (the mill) and the **Gary Land Company** (the town). Roughly **$65 million** was committed to plant and townsite.
- The first building was the Gary Land Company office (April 1906), which doubled as schoolhouse, post office, and town hall. The company built only **506 permanent houses** in the "First Subdivision" between **1906 and 1909** — nowhere near enough; thousands of workers lived in shacks and tents beside the construction.
- Population: **5,000–6,000 by 1908**; about **55,000 by 1920**; **over 100,000 by 1930**, with more than **50 nationalities** represented. Boosters called it the **"Magic City"** and **"City of the Century."** By **1930**, immigrants and their children were **45%** of the population, Black Southerners nearly **18%**, and over **9,000 Mexicans** lived in Gary and East Chicago.
- Gary was the epicenter of the **Great Steel Strike of 1919**. Gary Works grew into the **largest steel mill complex in North America**.
- Peak population **~178,000 in 1960**; by the **2020 census, 69,093** — a decline of **61% since 1960**, among the steepest collapses of any American city, as steel restructured and jobs moved offshore.

**Hershey, Pennsylvania — the sweet spot (1903–1937)**
- **1894** — **Milton S. Hershey** founded the Hershey Chocolate Company as a subsidiary of his Lancaster Caramel Company; he sold the caramel business in **1900** for **$1 million** to bet everything on chocolate. ("Caramels are just a fad, but chocolate is a permanent thing.")
- **1903** — construction began on a chocolate factory at his birthplace, **Derry Church, Pennsylvania**; the plant opened in **1905** and the town was renamed **Hershey in 1906**. Workers got brick houses with lawns they could actually buy, plus parks, a school, a trolley system, and **Hersheypark** (opened **1906** as an employee recreation ground). The factory was famously built **without windows** so workers wouldn't be distracted.
- **1909** — childless, Hershey founded the **Hershey Industrial School** for orphaned boys. In **November 1918**, three years after his wife Catherine's death, he quietly transferred essentially his entire fortune — about **$60 million** in company stock — into the **Hershey Trust** for the school. The secret bequest was exposed by the New York Times in **1923**; by the 2000s the trust was worth **$5–10 billion**.
- **April 2, 1937** — after Depression-era wage cuts (despite **$37 million in profits from 1930–1936**), CIO-organized workers — **400 to 600+** of them — launched a **sit-down strike** inside the factory, echoing the GM Flint sit-down of the previous winter. Hershey's appeal that each orphan cost **$1,000 a year** to educate backfired; workers retorted they were worth "at least as much as two orphans."
- **April 7, 1937** — a mob of **~3,000** angry dairy farmers (whose milk was spoiling unsold) and loyalist employees stormed the plant with clubs, beating the sit-down strikers out; about **25 strikers were injured**. Loyalists later paraded with signs reading **"CIO Can't Make a Monkey Out of Us."** The strike broke the paternal spell; Hershey, then 80, never recovered from the betrayal he felt. He died in **1945**; the Hershey Trust still controls the company.

**Peak and decline**
- At their peak there were **more than 2,500 company towns** in the U.S., housing roughly **3% of the population**. A **1916 Bureau of Labor Statistics** sample of 213 companies found **34.4%** of their workers lived in company housing.
- **1933** — the New Deal's Cotton Textile Code (under the NIRA) declared there was **"something feudal and repugnant to American principles in the practice of employer ownership of employee homes."** Though the Supreme Court struck down the NIRA in **1935**, the **Wagner Act** passed the same year guaranteed union rights — union membership **tripled in eight years** — and the **Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938** set a federal minimum wage and finished off wage-scrip.
- **January 7, 1946** — the Supreme Court ruled **5–3 in Marsh v. Alabama** that constitutional rights apply inside company towns. A Jehovah's Witness, Grace Marsh, had been arrested for trespassing while handing out religious literature on the sidewalk of **Chickasaw, Alabama**, a Gulf Shipbuilding Corporation town. Justice Hugo Black: ownership does not "give municipalities... the right to restrict" fundamental liberties — the more an owner opens property to public use, the more his rights are circumscribed by the public's rights.
- The automobile finished the job: by the **1920s–30s**, workers could live miles from the plant, shop in real towns, and drive to a better job. Welfare capitalism curdled into something workers saw as demeaning.
- Survivals and afterlives: the Roebling family (steel cable for the Brooklyn and Golden Gate Bridges) sold its New Jersey town's houses to workers in **1947**. **Kannapolis, North Carolina** lived and died with Cannon Mills (Fieldcrest Cannon; mill closed **2003**). **Steinway Village** in Queens, NYC, began in **1870** when William Steinway bought **400 acres** for his piano works. **Corning, New York** is still effectively a company town. **Scotia, California** — Pacific Lumber's redwood town, founded **1863**, town burned in **1895** and rebuilt, Mill B once the world's largest sawmill — remained wholly company-owned until the firm's **2008 bankruptcy**; its **~270 homes** were only sold off to residents in the **2010s–2020s**, making it one of America's last true company towns.
- Modern echoes: tech campuses, Amazon warehouse towns, Elon Musk's **Starbase, Texas** (incorporated **2025**), and Nevada's failed **"Innovation Zones"** proposal (**2021**) that would have let corporations form their own county governments — the company town idea keeps coming back.

## Angle / hook

Your employer owned your house, ran the only store, picked the preacher, banned the beer, and paid you in money no one else would take. For a century, thousands of American towns weren't towns at all — they were corporate property with zip codes, an experiment in industrial feudalism that built Lowell's spindles, Hershey's chocolate empire, and U.S. Steel's skyline — and ended, at Pullman and Blair Mountain, in gunfire.

### Episode-specific texture to exploit

- Open on the paycheck, not the factory: a Pullman worker in May 1894 whose pay envelope, after the company deducted rent for the house he was forbidden to buy, contained pennies. Hold on that image.
- The $3 Pullman library fee (~$100 today) for a library the workers couldn't afford, in a town built "for" them.
- The Greenstone Church standing empty for five years because no congregation could pay George Pullman's $300/month rent — in a town of 12,000 souls.
- Alcohol banned for workers, poured freely at the Hotel Florence for railroad executives. One bar in town, and you weren't invited.
- Jennie Curtis, the Pullman seamstress — real, documented strike participant; her house still stands in the national park. A face for the story.
- Merle Travis's brother John's letter: "another day older and deeper in debt." A song lyric that is literally a family document.
- Hershey workers in 1937 telling the kindest boss in America they were worth "at least as much as two orphans."
- Dairy farmers clubbing chocolate workers in 1937 because the cows don't stop milking for a strike.
- Gary going from sand dunes to 55,000 people in 14 years — and losing 61% of its people after 1960. The company town that became a city and then a cautionary tale.
- Grace Marsh, arrested on a sidewalk in 1946 for handing out pamphlets in a town her employer didn't own — but Gulf Shipbuilding did. The Supreme Court sided with her 5–3.
- George Pullman's lead-lined, concrete-encased grave. The workers outlived him; his family wasn't sure the grave would.
- The 1898 Illinois Supreme Court order: the state itself forced America's most famous company to sell its town.
