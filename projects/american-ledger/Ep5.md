## Topic research

**The world before the fall**

- **November 23, 1900** — Youngstown Sheet & Tube Company founded in Youngstown, Ohio. It grows into one of the largest steelmakers in America; at its peak it is the largest corporation headquartered in Ohio.
- **1802** — the Mahoning Valley's first blast furnace opens, around the same time Ohio becomes a state. Local iron ore, coal, and the Mahoning River make the valley a natural steel corridor.
- **1930 census** — Youngstown's population peaks at **170,002**, making it a mid-sized American industrial city roughly equidistant (65 miles) from Cleveland and Pittsburgh.
- **1950** — Youngstown Sheet & Tube employs roughly **27,000** people. The home plant, the Campbell Works in Campbell/Struthers, runs **four blast furnaces, twelve open hearth furnaces, two Bessemer converters, a 79-inch hot strip mill**, and seamless tube mills. The Brier Hill Works runs two blast furnaces named **Grace and Jeannette**.
- **1960** — city population still **166,689**. Steel is the economy, the tax base, and the identity.

**Absentee ownership — the setup**

- **1969** — **Lykes Corporation**, a New Orleans-based shipping conglomerate one-sixth the size of Sheet & Tube, buys the company. Justice Department investigators warn the steel company will not survive the deal; they are overruled by U.S. Attorney General **John Mitchell** (later of Watergate notoriety). Lykes loads up on acquisitions and does not modernize Youngstown's aging open-hearth furnaces — technology already being replaced worldwide by basic oxygen and electric furnaces, including in postwar-rebuilt German and Japanese mills.

**Black Monday**

- **September 19, 1977, just before 7 a.m.** — workers arrive for the early shift at the Campbell Works with lunch pails and hard hats, talking about that night's Steelers Monday Night Football game. Youngstown Sheet & Tube — now a Lykes subsidiary — announces it is closing most of the Campbell Works and concentrating production at its Indiana Harbor Works near Chicago. **Approximately 5,000 workers are furloughed in a single day**, many of them third-generation steelworkers with 20–30 years in the mill. The WARN Act (60-day layoff notice) is still **eleven years away**; no notice is legally required. The day becomes known locally as **"Black Monday."**
- A young man named **Ulam** (New Castle, Pa.) shows up that same morning for a hiring interview; the guard tells him: "Go home, kid, it's all over."

**The fight back — and failure**

- **September–October 1977** — Catholic Bishop **James W. Malone** (Diocese of Youngstown) and Episcopal Bishop **John H. Burt** convene local clergy; the **Ecumenical Coalition of the Mahoning Valley** forms, eventually numbering **200+ religious leaders**.
- The coalition, steelworkers, labor lawyer **Staughton Lynd**, and economist Gar Alperovitz's team propose reopening the mill under **worker–community ownership**. A Carter-administration-funded feasibility study concludes a modernized mill could re-employ **4,000** people and operate at a profit. The **"Save Our Valley" campaign** opens Save Our Valley Accounts at local banks.
- **April 1979** — the worker-community buyout effort collapses; the funding is never assembled.

**The cascade**

- **1979** — Sheet & Tube's **Brier Hill Works** closes (about **1,500** jobs). Sheet & Tube's remains have been absorbed via the Lykes–LTV merger; Brier Hill passes to **Jones & Laughlin Steel**, then conglomerate **LTV**.
- **1979–1980** — **U.S. Steel** announces it will let its Youngstown district mills run down and pulls out of the area entirely — the **Ohio Works** and **McDonald Works** — eliminating roughly **3,500–4,000** more jobs. The announcement lands one day before **Jones & Laughlin** discloses its own Mahoning Valley cuts.
- **1981** — **Republic Steel** phases out the last basic steel operations at its **Haselton** plant; the last blast furnace in Youngstown proper goes cold. Republic later files for bankruptcy in the mid-1980s.
- **April 28, 1982** — the last four standing blast furnaces at U.S. Steel's Ohio Works are **demolished**; wire photos of the collapse circle the world as the symbolic end of Mahoning Valley steelmaking.
- **1982** — Staughton Lynd publishes *The Fight Against Black Monday*.
- Total damage, per the standard accounting: about **10,000 steel jobs** at the three mills, and a regional loss of roughly **40,000 manufacturing jobs**, **400 satellite businesses**, **$414 million in personal income**, and **33–75% of school tax revenues**.

**The half-century after**

- Population, city of Youngstown: **1930: 170,002 → 1970: 139,788 → 1980: 115,427 → 1990: 95,787 → 2000: 82,026 → 2010: 66,982 → 2020: 60,068**. A loss of nearly **two-thirds** from peak — roughly **110,000 people gone**.
- **2020 census** — median household income: **$30,129**, less than half the national figure. Mahoning County median household income (2019 data): **$46,042** vs. Ohio's **$56,602**.
- **1966–2019** — GM's **Lordstown Assembly** (opened 1966, peak employment up to **13,000**, long about 8,000) becomes the valley's largest industrial employer after steel dies. GM ends production **March 6, 2019**; the last **1,500** workers lose their jobs. The plant passes to Lordstown Motors (2019), then Foxconn (2022); in 2025 the property is sold to a reported SoftBank affiliate for AI data-center equipment.
- What exists now: **Vallourec Star** runs a pipe mill on part of the old Brier Hill site (the works briefly reopened in **1986** as North Star Steel under Cargill; Cargill sold it in 2002); **Youngstown State University** and **Mercy Health** are anchor employers; **America Makes**, the national additive-manufacturing institute, is headquartered downtown. Largest industrial employers inside city limits: Vallourec Star and Trivium Packaging.
- **1995** — Bruce Springsteen releases *The Ghost of Tom Joad*, with the song **"Youngstown"** — the city's epitaph in the national imagination.

## Angle / hook

On a Monday morning in September 1977, five thousand men walked into the Campbell Works before the 7 a.m. shift — and by the end of the day the mill that had employed their fathers and grandfathers simply no longer existed. No fire, no strike, no bankruptcy hearing: just a statement from a New Orleans shipping conglomerate. This is what deindustrialization looked like from street level — and Youngstown is where the Rust Belt got its name.
