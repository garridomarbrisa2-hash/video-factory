## Topic research

- **1853** — Congress authorizes the War Department, under Secretary of War **Jefferson Davis**, to survey five potential transcontinental routes; a massive **twelve-volume report** lands in early **1855**, but sectional politics blocks every bill until the Southern states secede in **1861**.
- **July 1, 1862** — In the middle of the Civil War, President **Abraham Lincoln** signs the **Pacific Railway Act of 1862**, chartering the **Union Pacific** (building west from the Missouri River near Omaha) and the **Central Pacific** (building east from Sacramento).
- The subsidy structure: **30-year federal bonds at 6% interest**, issued per mile of completed track — **$16,000 per mile** on the plains, **$32,000 per mile** on the plateau between the Rockies and the Sierra Nevada, and **$48,000 per mile** in the mountain ranges (mountain rate capped at **300 miles** by Section 11 of the Act).
- Land grants: initially **ten alternating sections per side per mile** — about **12,800 acres for every mile of track** (doubled from the original 6,400) — in a checkerboard pattern, the government keeping the sections in between.
- **January 8, 1863** — Central Pacific president **Leland Stanford** (who is simultaneously **governor of California**) turns the first shovelful of earth in Sacramento.
- **1864** — The **Pacific Railway Act of 1864** doubles the land grant, lets companies collect bond loans twice as fast, and — critically — demotes the government's claim from first mortgage to second, letting the railroads sell their own first-mortgage bonds to private investors.
- The Central Pacific is controlled by four Sacramento merchants — the **"Big Four"**: **Leland Stanford** (president), **Collis P. Huntington** (Washington lobbyist), **Mark Hopkins** (treasurer), **Charles Crocker** (construction boss). Their own construction fronts (Charles Crocker & Co.) mirror the Union Pacific's scheme.
- **1864–1867** — Union Pacific vice president **Thomas C. Durant** and partners route contracts through **Crédit Mobilier of America**, a paper construction company they own, billing the railroad — meaning, in large part, the federal loan money — inflated prices. Durant deliberately meanders the UP route to add subsidized miles.
- Labor costs, west: Chinese workers are first hired in **1865 at $26/month** for a six-day week, paying for their own food and lodging; white workers got more pay plus free board. By **1866–1867**, **10,000–15,000 Chinese laborers** make up roughly **90% of the Central Pacific workforce**. In spring **1867** wages rise from **$31 to $35/month**; Chinese crews strike demanding **$40/month** and a ten-hour day — and lose.
- Labor costs, east: the Union Pacific workforce is built on **Irish immigrants**, Civil War veterans from both armies, ex-convicts, and Mormon contract crews. Crews average **two miles of track per day**, with a record **10-mile day** at Victory Camp, Utah, in **1869**.
- **1867–1868** — Crédit Mobilier congressman-shareholder **Oakes Ames** of Massachusetts sells **160 shares at the $100 par value** to nine representatives and two senators — stock that within weeks pays out bonds and dividends worth **more than $99 per $100 share**. Some congressmen pay out of future dividends. **$9 million** in discounted stock and cash goes toward buying Washington goodwill. Takers include Speaker **Schuyler Colfax** (by 1872, Grant's vice president) and Rep. **James Brooks**.
- The markup, in hard numbers: Congress paid **$94,650,287** to the Union Pacific and **$50,720,959** to Crédit Mobilier — a build that whistleblower accounting put at roughly **$53 million** of real work. Crédit Mobilier's arrangement generates **$43,929,328 in profit** (about **$1.1 billion in 2025 dollars**), of which directors publicly report only **$23,366,319.81**.
- **September 4, 1872** — The **New York Sun** breaks the Crédit Mobilier story mid-presidential campaign, fed by disgruntled insider **Henry Simpson McComb**. The **Poland Committee** (chaired by Rep. **Luke Poland** of Vermont) investigates; on **February 27, 1873**, the House merely **censures Ames and Brooks** — nobody is expelled, nobody goes to prison.
- **May 10, 1869** — The lines meet at **Promontory Summit, Utah Territory**. Stanford drives the ceremonial **17.6-karat golden spike**; telegraph wires attached to the spike carry the blows live — the first coast-to-coast "live" news event. Final tally: Union Pacific laid **1,087 miles**, Central Pacific **690 miles**. Total project cost: **$110–120 million** in 46 months.
- The overlooked scandal-before-the-scandal: hoping to maximize per-mile subsidies, the two companies graded **more than 200 miles of parallel, competing roadbed** across northern Utah before Washington forced the Promontory compromise in **April 1869**.
- Was the public paid back? The bonds were a **loan, not a gift** — the government held a mortgage on every mile and charged **6% interest**. The government loaned a total of **$64,623,512** to the transcontinental companies; **both railroads ultimately repaid the debt in full**, and the government also got reduced rates on military and freight traffic for decades.
- The land side: all told, federal railroad land grants ran about **129–130 million acres** (states added ~**50 million more**) across all railroads — land worth roughly **$1/acre at the time**, which the railroads later sold at an average of **$2.81/acre** to settlers.

## Angle / hook

Everyone knows the golden spike. Almost nobody asks who signed the checks — because the answer is uncomfortable: you did. The transcontinental railroad was sold as a patriotic sprint of engineering genius, but under the smoke it was a money machine: the government lent the cash, gifted the land, held the risk, and the men who ran the financing quietly siphoned off more profit than the men who swung the hammers ever saw. The greatest engineering project of the nineteenth century was also its first great corporate heist — and the strangest part is that, technically, the taxpayers got their money back.
