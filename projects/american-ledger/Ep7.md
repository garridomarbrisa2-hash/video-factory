## Topic research

- **The world before the 8-hour day:** during the Industrial Revolution the working day ran **10 to 16 hours**, the workweek was typically **six days**, and child labor was standard. As late as **1905**, even with the 8-hour day spreading in the printing trades, the majority of Americans still worked **12- to 14-hour days**. In steel, the prized 8-hour shift at Homestead was pushed back to **12 hours** after the union was crushed in 1892 — and skilled wages there fell by **one-fifth between 1892 and 1907** (labor historian David Brody, *Steelworkers in America: The Nonunion Era*).
- **1791** — Philadelphia carpenters strike for the **ten-hour day** — the first shot in a 147-year war. By the 1830s it is a general demand.
- **June 1835** — the **Philadelphia general strike**, the first general strike in North America, led by Irish coal heavers. Their banners read: *"From 6 to 6, ten hours work and two hours for meals."* Boston ship carpenters win an 8-hour day in **1842** — an outlier, not the rule.
- **1817** — the slogan's source: Welsh reformer **Robert Owen**, at his New Lanark mills, coins *"Eight hours' labour, eight hours' recreation, eight hours' rest."* In 1860s America, Boston machinist **Ira Steward** recast it as the line that would march through fifty years of strikes: **"Eight hours for work, eight hours for rest, eight hours for what we will."**
- **August 1866** — the **National Labor Union** convenes in **Baltimore** — the first national labor federation — and resolves: *"The first and great necessity of the present to free labor of this country from capitalist slavery, is the passing of a law by which eight hours shall be the normal working day in all States of the American Union."*
- **1867** — Illinois passes an 8-hour law with so many loopholes it is unenforceable. On **May 1, 1867**, a Chicago citywide strike shuts the city's economy for a week, then collapses — a dress rehearsal for 1886.
- **June 25, 1868** — Congress passes an **8-hour law for federal laborers and mechanics**, overriding President Andrew Johnson's veto. It is promptly gutted: wages are cut **~20%** to match the shorter day.
- **May 19, 1869** — President **Ulysses S. Grant** issues a proclamation barring wage cuts for federal day-workers under the 1868 law — the first presidential intervention in the hours fight.
- **1872** — roughly **100,000 New York City workers**, mostly building trades, strike for the 8-hour day and largely win it — proof the demand could succeed, trade by trade, when unions were strong.
- **1884** — the **Federation of Organized Trades and Labor Unions** (forerunner of the AFL), meeting in Chicago, sets the deadline: *"Eight hours shall constitute a legal day's labour from and after May 1, 1886."* Labor had two years to make it real.
- **The Knights of Labor**, under Grand Master Workman **Terence V. Powderly**, officially refuses to join the strike call — but local assemblies in Chicago, Cincinnati, and Milwaukee join anyway.
- **May 1, 1886** — the first great national strike for the 8-hour day. In Chicago, **Albert Parsons** leads **80,000 people** down Michigan Avenue — regarded as the first modern May Day parade — under the cry **"Eight-hour day with no cut in pay."** Nationwide, roughly **350,000 workers** strike at **~1,200 factories**: **70,000 in Chicago, 45,000 in New York, 32,000 in Cincinnati**. Some win 8 or 9 hours with no pay cut; others take wage cuts with the shorter day.
- **May 3, 1886** — at the **McCormick Harvesting Machine Company** works on Chicago's West Side, police fire into striking workers attacking strikebreakers. At least **two demonstrators are killed** (contemporary accounts claim two, four, or six), many wounded. August Spies, editor of the German-language *Arbeiter-Zeitung*, prints the circular headlined **"REVENGE! WORKINGMEN! TO ARMS!!!"** calling a protest meeting for the next evening.
- **May 4, 1886 — Haymarket Square, Chicago.** The rally on Des Plaines Street is peaceful; Mayor **Carter Harrison** attends and leaves satisfied. Around **10:20 PM**, as the crowd dwindles in drizzle, **176 police officers under Inspector John Bonfield** march in and order dispersal. As speaker **Samuel Fielden** steps down from the wagon, an unknown person throws a dynamite bomb into the police ranks. **Officer Mathias Degan** dies first — killed almost instantly. Police open fire; the crowd fires back. Final toll: **7 police officers dead** (from the bomb and police crossfire), **at least 4 civilians dead**, **60+ officers injured**, **70+ total injuries**, 100+ arrested. The bomber is never identified.
- **The trial:** eight anarchists are indicted for the murder of Officer Degan — **August Spies, Albert Parsons, Samuel Fielden, Michael Schwab, George Engel, Adolph Fischer, Oscar Neebe, Louis Lingg**. Only two were even at Haymarket that night; the state never claimed any of them threw the bomb. Judge **Joseph E. Gary** runs the trial; all 12 jurors admitted prejudice against the defendants. The jury convicts all eight on an unprecedented conspiracy theory: their words incited the unknown bomber. **Seven are sentenced to death; Neebe gets 15 years.** Parsons, who had fled, walks into court on day one and surrenders.
- **November 10, 1887** — Governor **Richard J. Oglesby** commutes **Fielden and Schwab** to life sentences after a petition with **41,000 Chicago signatures**. That same day, **Louis Lingg** — four bombs had been found in his cell on November 6 — kills himself by biting a smuggled dynamite blasting cap.
- **November 11, 1887** — **Spies, Parsons, Engel, and Fischer are hanged** at noon in the Cook County jail. Spies's last words: *"The time will come when our silence will be more powerful than the voices you strangle today."* Over **150,000 people** line the funeral route — the largest funeral procession in Chicago history to that date. The case is now regarded as one of the worst miscarriages of justice in American history.
- **June 26, 1893** — Governor **John Peter Altgeld** pardons the three surviving prisoners (Neebe, Fielden, Schwab), denouncing the trial as unfair and the evidence as fabricated. The pardon destroys his political career.
- **The May Day legacy:** inspired by the American 8-hour movement, the **Second International** in Paris (1889) calls for worldwide May 1 demonstrations starting 1890 — the birth of **International Workers' Day**. The holiday America invented is now celebrated everywhere except, officially, in America.
- **Between 1866 and 1892**, the **Pinkerton National Detective Agency** — Allan Pinkerton's private force, motto "We Never Sleep" — participates in **70 labor disputes** and opposes over **125,000 strikers** (PBS/American Experience figures). Local sheriffs were often too sympathetic to labor; industrialists hired Pinkertons instead.
- **1892 — Homestead, Pennsylvania.** **Andrew Carnegie**'s Carnegie Steel is the most profitable steel company on earth; the **Amalgamated Association of Iron and Steel Workers** is the strongest craft union in the industry. Carnegie retreats to his estate in **Scotland** and leaves chairman **Henry Clay Frick** — a veteran union-breaker — orders to cut the union out. Frick demands wage cuts (the sliding scale tied to steel prices), locks out the workers, and builds a **3-mile fence topped with barbed wire**, with rifle ports, around the works — the men call it "Fort Frick."
- **July 6, 1892 — the Battle of Homestead.** Around 3 AM, the tug *Little Bill* tows two barges carrying **~300 Pinkerton detectives** armed with Winchester rifles (a roster seized later showed **266 men, 360 rifles**, and provisions for a regiment for a week) up the Monongahela River toward the mill. A horseman rides through Homestead giving the alarm; **~10,000 strikers and townspeople** mass on the riverbank. Shots are exchanged — who fired first is still disputed; the *New York Times* said the Pinkertons. The firefight lasts all day: strikers bring up a small cannon (a misfire kills striker **Silas Wain**), try to set the barges alight with oil and burning rafts, and use July 4th fireworks as cover. Four times the Pinkertons raise a white flag; four times it is shot down. At **5 PM** they surrender, are marched through a gauntlet of the enraged town, and their barges are burned. **Toll: 7 workers and 3 Pinkertons dead** (Pinkerton's own testimony; total dead on both sides commonly given as ~10–16 across the whole clash), dozens wounded on both sides — **33 to 35 Pinkertons** hospitalized.
- **July 12, 1892** — **8,500 Pennsylvania National Guard** troops, armed with the latest rifles and Gatling guns, march into Homestead at Frick's request and hold the town. Strikebreakers move in behind fixed bayonets.
- **July 23, 1892** — anarchist **Alexander Berkman** (no connection to the strikers; lover of Emma Goldman) walks into Frick's Pittsburgh office and shoots him twice in the neck and stabs him three times. Frick, stab wounds bleeding, tackles Berkman with help and is back at his desk within a week. Public sympathy for the strike evaporates overnight.
- **September 30, 1892** — **33 steelworkers are charged with treason** against the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania — for a strike. None are convicted, but the legal cost bleeds the union dry.
- **November 20, 1892** — the Amalgamated Association votes to return to work. The union is effectively destroyed; Frick cables Carnegie in Scotland: *"Our victory is complete."* Carnegie — who publicly claimed to support unions in his essays — never apologized. Steel stays non-union for the next **40 years**, until the 1930s.
- **1893–94** — the worst depression in American history to that date throws millions out of work. In the planned company town of **Pullman, Illinois**, just south of Chicago, **George Pullman** — maker of the palace sleeping car — cuts wages by an average of **~25%** (some workers' pay fell far more) while **refusing to cut rents** in the company-owned town, where housing cost more than in neighboring Chicago. Workers later testified of paychecks of a few cents after rent was deducted.
- **May 11, 1894, 9:00 AM** — after three grievance-committee members are fired, **~3,000–4,000 Pullman workers walk out** — against the advice of their own union leadership. The town's shops, owned by the company, extend no credit to strikers.
- **June 26, 1894** — **Eugene V. Debs**'s **American Railway Union** — ~150,000 members, one of the largest unions in the country — votes to **boycott**: no ARU member will handle any train carrying a Pullman car. Within days, **125,000 to 250,000 railroad workers in 27 states** have joined; rail traffic west of Chicago seizes up. Debs sends **~4,000 telegrams** in the boycott's first week begging locals to stay nonviolent.
- The railroads' **General Managers' Association** deliberately attaches Pullman cars to **mail trains** — converting a labor boycott into federal obstruction. Attorney General **Richard Olney** — himself a former railroad lawyer still holding a railroad directorship — gets a sweeping federal injunction on **July 2, 1894**, citing the Sherman Antitrust Act and mail obstruction. President **Grover Cleveland** orders federal troops to Chicago over the furious objection of Illinois Governor **John Peter Altgeld** (the same man who had pardoned the Haymarket survivors the year before), who called it unconstitutional.
- **July 4, 1894** — violence erupts. Mobs burn hundreds of railcars; fires destroy several vacant buildings of the **World's Columbian Exposition** (the White City fairgrounds). Railroad property damage is estimated at **$340,000** in Chicago alone.
- **The clampdown:** by mid-July Chicago holds **~6,000 federal and state troops, 3,100 police, and 5,000 deputy marshals** — deputies sworn in and paid by the railroads themselves. On **July 7**, after being assaulted, guardsmen fire into a mob, killing **between 4 and 30 people** (estimates vary by source). Total deaths across the strike: **~30–34 people** commonly cited (some estimates run to 70); 53 seriously wounded in Chicago. Federal or state troops deploy in **Nebraska, Iowa, California, Colorado, Oklahoma, and Illinois**.
- **July 10–17, 1894** — Debs and three other ARU officers are arrested for violating the injunction; police ransack the ARU office and seize Debs's personal mail. The strike, leaderless and facing bayonets, collapses. Troops are recalled **July 20**; the Pullman plant reopens **August 2** with strikebreakers; the ARU is dissolved.
- **Debs serves six months** in the McHenry County jail at Woodstock, Illinois, for contempt — the Supreme Court upholds the injunction power in *In re Debs* (1895). In jail he reads Marx and emerges a socialist; he will run for President five times — once, in **1920**, from a federal prison cell, winning **~900,000 votes**.
- **June 28, 1894** — six days after the boycott begins, and with the strike still raging, Cleveland and Congress rush **Labor Day** into law as a federal holiday — a peace offering to labor, and a deliberate choice of September over the radical, Haymarket-tinged May 1. America's official workers' holiday exists because of a strike the government was simultaneously crushing with troops.
- **1914** — the business case appears. On **January 5, 1914**, the **Ford Motor Company** announces **$5 for an 8-hour day** — more than double the prevailing **$2.34 for nine hours**. 10,000 men riot-apply at Highland Park the next morning in freezing cold. Turnover collapses; productivity jumps; Ford's **profit margin doubles from $30 million to $60 million within two years** (1914–1916). The $5 day was not charity — it was the cheapest way Ford found to stabilize an assembly line that had been burning through workers.
- **1916 — the first federal law.** After railroad brotherhoods demand 8 hours plus time-and-a-half (**fall 1915**), the railroads reject them (**mid-June 1916**), and the brotherhoods set a national strike deadline of **September 4, 1916** — which would have strangled war preparedness. On **August 28, 1916**, President **Woodrow Wilson** asks Congress to legislate. On **September 3, 1916**, the **Adamson Act** passes: 8 hours is the standard day for interstate railroad workers, with overtime — **the first federal law regulating hours of workers in private companies**. On **March 19, 1917**, the Supreme Court upholds it **5–4** in *Wilson v. New*.
- **1926 — the weekend arrives.** Henry Ford, who had decided on a shorter week as early as **1922**, puts his factory workers on a **five-day, 40-hour week effective May 1, 1926**, extending it to office staff that **August**; the formal public establishment is reported in the *New York Times* **September 26, 1926**. Ford's stated logic is hard business, not sentiment: shorter hours mean more output per hour, and — his heresy — workers with leisure become customers. Ford: *"It is high time to rid ourselves of the notion that leisure for workmen is either 'lost time' or a class privilege."* Edsel Ford had said it plainer in 1922: every man needs more than one day a week for rest and family. Manufacturers across the country follow; the Monday-to-Friday week becomes the American standard.
- **1938 — the finish line.** The **Fair Labor Standards Act**, championed by Labor Secretary **Frances Perkins** (the first woman in a US cabinet) and pushed through in its final form after the Black-Connery bill's fiercer version (40¢ wage, 40-hour week) was watered down under Southern pressure, is signed by **Franklin D. Roosevelt on June 25, 1938** and takes effect **October 24, 1938**. It sets: a federal **minimum wage of 25 cents an hour** (≈$5.50 in today's money); a maximum workweek of **44 hours** the first year, stepping down to **42**, then **40 hours by 1940**; **time-and-a-half overtime** beyond the weekly maximum; and a ban on "oppressive child labor" — no manufacturing or mining work under **16**, no hazardous jobs under **18**. It initially covers only about **one-fifth of the labor force** — but the principle is now federal law: the 8-hour day and the 40-hour week belong to everyone the commerce clause can reach. It is considered the last major law of the New Deal — signed 52 years, one month, and 21 days after Haymarket.

## Angle / hook

Your weekend has a body count. The 8-hour day — the thing every American worker now treats as a birthright — took fifty years of war to win: a bomb in a Chicago square, barges of hired gunmen on the Monongahela, federal troops in the rail yards, four men hanged for a crime nobody proved. And in the end, it wasn't a strike or a statute that sealed the deal — it was Henry Ford discovering that rested workers made him richer. This is the receipt for your Saturday.
