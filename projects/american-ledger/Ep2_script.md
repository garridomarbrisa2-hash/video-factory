# Ep2 Script — $75 Million Broke America: The Panic Wall Street Wants Forgotten

global_style: ledger

## HOOK

In March 1792, America had seventy-five million dollars in debt and one man gambling all of it on Wall Street.

Within thirty days the entire financial system stopped. Here's the crash the textbooks skip.

His name was William Duer. He had been Alexander Hamilton's own Assistant Secretary of the Treasury.

He knew where every bond was priced, and he used that knowledge to blow up the market that had just been born.

## BODY

### THE SETUP (1790–1791)

In October 1790, Hamilton took the wreckage of Revolutionary War debt and reorganized it into two new federal bonds: the Sixes, paying six percent, and the Threes, paying three.

A year later, the Sixes still traded at seventy cents on the dollar — barely half of face value.

William Duer saw the gap and moved. In 1791, he partnered with Alexander Macomb, one of the richest men in New York, to corner the market in US debt securities.

Buy low. Squeeze the float. Force the price up.

On July 1, 1791, scrip for shares in the new Bank of the United States went on sale at twenty-five dollars a right. The city went into a buying frenzy.

By July 31 — thirty days later — the Sixes hit par: one hundred.

Duer wasn't done. That November, he drew on funds from the Society for Establishing Useful Manufactures, Hamilton's industrial project in Paterson, New Jersey.

He personally governed that company and had raised five hundred thousand dollars to capitalize it. Now he used it as his own line of credit.

### THE SQUEEZE (December 1791 – March 1792)

Duer's circle called themselves the Six Percent Club. Between December 1791 and January 1792, they drove the six percent bonds from one hundred ten to one hundred twenty-five.

Bank of the United States shares rose from five hundred twenty-eight dollars to seven hundred twelve in the same weeks.

The Bank noticed. On December 29, 1791, it held seven hundred six thousand dollars in cash reserves.

By January 31, 1792: five hundred ten thousand. By March 9: two hundred forty-four thousand — a two-thirds drain in ten weeks.

So the Bank slammed the brakes. Discounts were cut from two point six eight million dollars to two point zero five million.

Credit vanished exactly when Duer needed to roll over his loans.

The bonds peaked at one hundred twenty-nine on March 5, 1792. That same week, the comptroller at the Society for Establishing Useful Manufactures discovered Duer's unauthorized loan.

He demanded it back. Duer had nowhere left to borrow.

### THE CRASH (March 9–23, 1792)

In three days — March 8 to March 9 — the Sixes fell from one hundred twenty-five point eight three to one hundred sixteen point two five.

On March 9, William Duer stopped paying his debts.

Two days later he wrote to Hamilton, blaming the collapse on notes "issued by my agent during my absence from this city."

Hamilton wrote back on March 14: "Act with fortitude and honor."

Duer didn't get the chance. On March 23, 1792, he was arrested and sent to the New Gaol debtors' prison in Manhattan.

Mobs surrounded the building. Inside, one creditor collected fifteen hundred dollars from him at gunpoint.

The total damage from Duer's collapse: three million dollars. A contemporary put it plainly — there was, he said, "not a rich man in the City" left standing.

Alexander Macomb defaulted on five hundred thousand dollars in stock purchases and joined Duer in prison. One associate had personally cosigned over two hundred thousand dollars of Duer's notes.

Hamilton didn't wait for Congress. On March 19, 1792, he began what historians now call America's first lender-of-last-resort operation.

Treasury purchases of government securities on the open market, hundreds of thousands of dollars pushed into banks across the Northeast. By May, the market had stabilized.

Two months after the crash, on May 17, 1792, twenty-four brokers signed the Buttonwood Agreement outside 68 Wall Street — partly to shut speculators like Duer out of the market for good.

That agreement became the founding document of the New York Stock Exchange.

William Duer stayed in debtors' prison for seven years. He died there on May 7, 1799. He never paid a cent of what he owed.

### THE SECOND PANIC (1811–1819)

Twenty-seven years later, the country did the same thing to itself — at national scale.

In 1811, Congress let the charter of the First Bank of the United States expire. For five years there was no central bank at all, and state banks printed paper without limit.

In 1816, Congress chartered a Second Bank of the United States. It opened in Philadelphia in January 1817 under president William Jones, eventually running eighteen branches with almost no oversight.

A boom followed. War-torn Europe needed American cotton, wheat, corn, and tobacco. Cotton rose from a few cents a pound to thirty-three cents in 1818.

The federal government sold public land at two dollars an acre, quarter down, the rest on credit — and speculators borrowed hard against it.

Public land debt owed to the government ballooned from three million dollars to seventeen million between 1815 and 1818.

In Madison County, Alabama, land that sold for thirty-five dollars an acre in 1817 went for one hundred thirty-four dollars an acre in 1818 — a two hundred seventy percent jump in a single year.

Then the Second Bank reversed course to protect its own reserves. Loans were cut from forty-three million dollars in early 1818 to thirty-one million by September 1819.

Note circulation fell from ten million to four million.

At the same time, British mills switched to cheaper Indian cotton and European harvests recovered.

In January 1819, the price of cotton dropped twenty-five percent in a single day. By autumn it was down more than half from its March peak.

Langdon Cheves took over as Bank president in 1819 and enforced the credit cuts without mercy.

State banks that couldn't redeem their notes in specie failed outright. Foreclosures swept the cotton belt — in the Huntsville region of Alabama alone, more than four hundred thousand acres were forfeited or sold off.

The human cost showed up in the numbers. Philadelphia committed one thousand eight hundred eight people to debtors' prison. Boston committed three thousand five hundred.

New York State property values fell from three hundred fifteen million dollars in 1818 to two hundred fifty-six million by 1820.

Pennsylvania land dropped from one hundred fifty dollars an acre in 1815 to thirty-five dollars in 1819.

New York City counted eight thousand paupers in 1819, out of a population of one hundred twenty thousand — thirteen thousand a year later.

Across New York, Philadelphia, and Baltimore combined, roughly fifty thousand people were out of work.

The depression dragged on into 1821. Agricultural prices fell by half.

And in a final irony, the Supreme Court decided McCulloch versus Maryland in 1819 — upholding the Bank's constitutionality at the exact moment the country learned to hate it.

The resentment didn't fade. It seeded Andrew Jackson's Bank War in the 1830s and the Bank's death when its charter expired in 1836.

## CTA / CLOSER

Two panics, twenty-seven years apart. The first one fit inside a single month and ended with one man in prison.

The second one took years, ruined tens of thousands of families, and rewrote how Americans thought about banks for a generation.

Both started the same way: cheap credit, a rising price, and someone convinced the rise would never stop.

Both ended the same way too — with a central bank pulling money out of the system faster than it had put it in.

Wall Street got its first stock exchange out of the wreckage of 1792. The country got its first taste of a national depression out of 1819.

Neither lesson stuck for long. The ledger doesn't forget. It just waits for the next name to fill in the blank.
