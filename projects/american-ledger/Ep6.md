## Topic research

- **1790** — First US census counts **697,624 enslaved people**, concentrated on Chesapeake tobacco and Carolina rice plantations. Six slave states. US cotton output is trivial: under **9,000 bales** a year (a bale = a standardized cube of roughly 500 lbs of fiber).
- **1793** — Eli Whitney, a 28-year-old Yale graduate working as a tutor on Catharine Greene's Mulberry Grove plantation in Georgia, builds the cotton gin ("gin" = engine): wire teeth on a rotating cylinder pull short-staple upland cotton fiber through slots too narrow for the seeds. Patent filed 1793, granted **March 14, 1794**.
- The labor math: one person hand-cleaning cotton separated seeds from about **1 pound of fiber in a 10-hour day**. Two people with a hand gin could clean **~50 pounds** in the same time. A single enslaved worker with a gin produced ~5 lbs of lint a day by hand-picking quality standards — later steam gins reached **~2,500 lbs/day** by the Civil War.
- Whitney made almost nothing from the patent. Planters copied the simple design ("patent piracy"); his lawsuits consumed his profits. He got rich instead on **interchangeable-parts musket contracts** for the US Army, starting 1798. He died in **1825** — before seeing the full empire his machine built.
- **The paradox:** the gin cut the labor of *processing* cotton but multiplied the labor of *growing and picking* it. Cotton became so profitable that demand for enslaved field labor exploded.
- **1800** — US production passes **200,000 bales** (~73 million lbs by some counts); by **1805**, from 2 million lbs (1793-era) to over **60 million pounds** a decade after the gin.
- **1808** — Congress bans the African slave trade (Constitution's earliest allowed date). Between 1790 and the ban, Southerners imported roughly **80,000 more Africans**. After 1808, the cotton labor force grows by natural increase and internal trade.
- **The Second Middle Passage:** roughly **one million enslaved people** were sold or forcibly marched from the Upper South (Virginia, Maryland, the Carolinas) to the cotton belt of Alabama, Mississippi, Louisiana, and Texas between the Revolution and the Civil War — the largest forced migration in American history. Coffles walked hundreds of miles; families were split at auction blocks in Richmond and New Orleans.
- Cotton exports: under **500,000 lbs in 1793** → **93 million lbs by 1810**. From **1820 to 1860, cotton was over half the value of all US exports** — the country's chief export for forty straight years.
- **1830s** — Annual US production exceeds **500 million pounds**. By **1830**, finished cotton goods were also the nation's largest industrial export. By mid-century, the South grew roughly **75% of the world's cotton**.
- **1850** — Enslaved population passes **3.2 million** (from ~700,000 in 1790). European and New England mill purchases climb from **720,000 bales (1830) to 2.85 million bales (1850)**.
- **1860** — The eighth census counts **3,953,760 enslaved people** — nearly one in three Southerners, and 15 slave states against 6 in 1790. About **2.5 million** of the enslaved worked the cotton regions from the Atlantic coastal plain to Texas.
- **1860** — US cotton output: nearly **4–4.8 million bales** (~2 billion pounds). Cotton accounts for almost **60% of all American exports by value — nearly $200 million a year** (roughly $5.7 billion in 2024 dollars). Cotton goods = 38% of Britain's exports; cotton was Britain's largest industry, generating ~12% of British national income in 1861.
- **The supply chain:** bales moved by steamboat down the Mississippi to New Orleans, or out of Charleston, Savannah, and Mobile — then to **Liverpool**, the greatest cotton port on earth, then by rail to the mills of **Manchester and Lancashire**. In 1861 the US supplied roughly **77–80% of the 800 million pounds of cotton Britain consumed**; American cotton also fed **90% of France's** mills, **60% of the Zollverein's (Germany)**, **92% of Russia's**.
- **The Northern cut:** New York City was "the capital of the South." Historians estimate New York captured roughly **40 cents of every cotton dollar** through shipping, insurance, financing, and selling goods back to planters — a trade worth about **$200 million annually**. Southern planters bought on New York credit; cotton factors took mortgages on plantations *and on the enslaved people themselves* as collateral. New York-based slave ships even ran the illegal post-1808 trade to Cuba and Brazil in the 1850s. Firms later woven into Wall Street's fabric (Lehman Brothers began as Alabama cotton brokers; Brown Brothers Harriman and New York Life have acknowledged cotton/slave-era ties; Aetna and New York Life sold policies insuring enslaved people) grew inside this system.
- **March 4, 1858** — Senator **James Henry Hammond** of South Carolina tells the US Senate: *"Without firing a gun, without drawing a sword, should they make war on us, we could bring the whole world to our feet... No power on the earth dares to make war upon it. Cotton is king."*
- **1861** — Secession. Confederate strategy ("King Cotton diplomacy"): an informal embargo — planters withhold and burn cotton (some **2.5 million bales** were burned) — to force British and French recognition. Exports to Europe collapse from **~3–3.8 million bales in 1860 to virtually nothing by 1862**. Jefferson Davis bets the republic on it.
- **Why it failed:** bumper crops of 1859–60 left Britain holding a **~1 million bale surplus**; the "cotton famine" didn't bite until late **1862** — after Antietam and the Emancipation Proclamation had reframed the war. Britain declared neutrality **May 1861** and never recognized the Confederacy. Egypt, India (production +70% in the 1860s), and Brazil (exports up 400%) filled the gap. The **Lancashire Cotton Famine** threw hundreds of thousands of mill hands out of work — in a trade directly employing **~600,000** and sustaining nearly **4 million** Britons (one-sixth of England's population) — but the 84% of the economy not tied to cotton prospered. The Union blockade, declared **April 19, 1861**, strangled what the embargo hadn't.
- **1865** — Defeat and emancipation destroy the system: roughly **$3–4 billion in "slave property"** (more capital than all Northern factories and railroads combined, per historians' estimates) is wiped off the books by the Thirteenth Amendment. Cotton output craters.
- **Sharecropping:** with no cash, no banks, and freedpeople holding no land, the South rebuilds on the **crop-lien system**. The landlord or furnishing merchant advances seed, tools, food, and mules at interest rates often of **25–50%+**, against the crop; the tenant owes a share — commonly **half** — at harvest. Merchants manipulated scales and ledgers; contracts often barred saving seed. Most sharecroppers ended each year deeper in debt — debt peonage in all but name, enforced by law courts that sided with landlords. Cotton remained king; the crown just changed hands.
- Recovery was slow: US production didn't surpass its **1860 peak until the late 1870s**, on the backs of Black sharecroppers and white tenant farmers alike.
- **1892** — The **boll weevil** (*Anthonomus grandis*), a quarter-inch beetle from Mexico, crosses the Rio Grande near **Brownsville, Texas**. It advances **40–160 miles per year**; by **1922** it infests the entire Cotton Belt from Texas to the Atlantic.
- Damage: the weevil cut local cotton yields by up to **50% within five years** of arrival. Texas alone lost an estimated **700,000 bales in 1904** (≈ **$42 million**); the state's yield reduction ran ~6% in 1910 and leaped to **34% in 1921**. At one point, **one-third of all insecticide used in the US** was aimed at boll weevils.
- The weevil (plus post-WWI price collapse, then the Depression) shattered the cotton tenancy economy. Counties hit by the weevil saw tenancy rates fall and Black population decline — one push factor among several behind the **Great Migration** of millions of Black Southerners to Northern cities, **1916–1970**. Mechanization after WWII finished what the weevil started; sharecropping effectively ended by the **1960s**.

## Angle / hook

In 1793, a Yankee schoolteacher spent ten days tinkering in a Georgia plantation workshop — and the machine he built did the opposite of what labor-saving machines are supposed to do: it made slavery grow. Within two generations, one crop tied the wealth of New York banks, Liverpool docks, and Manchester mills to the cotton rows of Mississippi — a single world system built on four million enslaved Americans. When the South bet that system could win a war, it found out the hard way what a commodity is actually worth.
