# Ep1 Script — How America Went From $75 Million in Debt to Half the World's Output

global_style: ledger

## HOOK

In 1790, the United States owed $75 million it couldn't pay. One hundred and sixty-two years later, it produced half of everything on Earth. That gap was not smooth, and it was not inevitable. It was built, broken, and rebuilt — at least a dozen times.

Here is every machine that made America rich, and what killed each one.

## BODY

### THE FIRST MACHINE: CREDIT (1790s)

Take the number itself. Seventy-five million dollars. For a nation of four million farmers, that debt was absurd. There was no central bank. No standing tax system. Just thirteen state treasuries that couldn't agree on anything.

Alexander Hamilton saw the weakness as a weapon.

In January 1790, he sent Congress the Report on Public Credit. His argument was simple: assume every state war debt — twenty-five million dollars of it — and fold it into one federal obligation. Force the creditors to buy American.

The South hated it. They'd already paid their debts. Hamilton had to trade them the permanent capital — moved from New York to a swamp on the Potomac — to get the vote.

He won. And in February 1791, he chartered the First Bank of the United States with ten million dollars in capital. That was larger than every other American corporation combined.

The credit machine was built. Now it needed something to fund.

### THE SECOND MACHINE: COTTON AND THE CANAL (1793–1825)

Eli Whitney's cotton gin, patented in 1793, was a hand-crank that let one worker clean fifty pounds of lint a day instead of five. The multiplier was brutal.

In 1790, the United States produced three thousand bales of cotton. By 1860, it was producing nearly four million — two-thirds of world supply. Cotton alone accounted for sixty percent of everything America sold abroad.

And the workforce that built it? Seven hundred thousand enslaved people in 1790, grew to four million by 1860. Every profit, every export figure, every bank deposit downstream of Liverpool — built on that.

Then came the Erie Canal. Three hundred sixty-three miles. Eighty-three locks. Seven million dollars. Dug by hand between 1817 and 1825. It cut freight costs from Buffalo to New York City from one hundred dollars a ton to six.

That machine stitched the Midwest to the Atlantic and turned New York into the nation's commercial capital.

### THE THIRD MACHINE: STEEL AND OIL (1862–1901)

The Civil War killed the cotton export machine. So America built a new one — out of iron.

The Homestead Act and the Pacific Railway Act, both 1862, gave away land and financed a railroad across a continent. The golden spike at Promontory Summit, Utah — May 10, 1869 — cut New York to San Francisco from months to seven days.

By 1900, two hundred thousand miles of track. That's five times what the country had in 1860.

On top of the rails sat the consolidators. Vanderbilt took the roads. Rockefeller's Standard Oil — founded 1870 — ended up controlling ninety percent of American refining. Carnegie's steel outfit fed the railroad, the bridge, the skyscraper.

In February 1901, Carnegie sold the company to J.P. Morgan for four hundred eighty million dollars. Morgan merged it into U.S. Steel — the first billion-dollar corporation in history.

Then came the bank panics. 1873. 1893. And in 1907, a panic so bad the government didn't fix it — J.P. Morgan did. Personally. From his library.

### THE FOURTH MACHINE: THE CENTRAL BANK AND THE ASSEMBLY LINE (1913–1929)

The lesson of 1907 was that one man couldn't referee the national economy forever. So on December 23, 1913, Woodrow Wilson signed the Federal Reserve Act. Twelve regional banks under a Washington board.

Two months earlier — October 1913, a few miles north of Detroit — Henry Ford had flipped on the moving assembly line.

Before Ford, building a Model T took twelve and a half hours. Afterward: ninety-three minutes. The price fell from eight hundred fifty dollars in 1908 to two hundred sixty dollars in 1924. Fifteen million sold.

Then World War One flipped America's position on the global ledger. The United States loaned the Allies roughly ten billion dollars. By 1918, it was no longer a borrower. It was the world's creditor — and for the first time, the largest economy on Earth.

The nineteen-twenties looked endless. The Dow climbed from sixty-three in 1921 to three hundred eighty-one on September 3, 1929.

Six weeks later, it wasn't.

### THE BREAK: 1929–1933

Black Thursday, October 24, 1929. Black Monday. Black Tuesday. The Dow didn't stop falling until July 8, 1932, when it closed at forty-one point two two.

That's an eighty-nine percent collapse. From peak to bottom.

By 1933, unemployment hit twenty-five percent. National income fell from eighty-seven point eight billion to forty point two billion. Of the twenty-five thousand banks in the United States, nine thousand failed. Depositors lost everything.

The credit machine, the factory, the stock market — all of it, at once, broke in front of everyone.

Franklin Roosevelt closed every bank in the country for four days in March 1933. Then he rebuilt the system around it. Glass-Steagall split commercial from investment banking. The FDIC guaranteed deposits. The SEC policed the markets. In 1935, the Social Security Act built the floor Americans — to this day — retire on.

### THE FIFTH MACHINE: WAR AND BRETTON WOODS (1940–1971)

Nothing fixed American industry forever except America at war.

Between 1940 and 1945, war production doubled GDP. Unemployment fell to one percent. Three hundred thousand aircraft rolled off assembly lines. Nineteen million women entered the workforce. National debt hit two hundred fifty-eight billion — about a hundred and twenty percent of GDP.

And in July 1944, forty-four nations gathered at Bretton Woods, New Hampshire, and rebuilt the entire world economy around the dollar. Peg to gold at thirty-five dollars an ounce. IMF. World Bank. The system that put Washington, not London, at the center of global finance.

By 1945, the United States produced nearly half of everything the world made.

The postwar machine ran clean for twenty-five years. The GI Bill sent millions to college. The Interstate Highway Act of 1956 — twenty-five billion dollars, forty-one thousand miles — was the largest public works project in American history. The top marginal tax rate sat at ninety-one percent.

Then on August 15, 1971, Richard Nixon closed the gold window.

The dollar — the anchor of the whole postwar system — was suddenly floating.

### THE LAST BREAK: RUST AND FINANCE (1973–2008)

The nineteen-seventies hurt. OPEC quadrupled oil prices. Inflation peaked near thirteen point five percent in 1980. Fed chairman Paul Volcker drove the federal funds rate to twenty percent in June 1981 to break it. He succeeded — but at the cost of two recessions.

And on September 19, 1977, Youngstown Sheet and Tube shut the Campbell Works. Five thousand jobs gone in one afternoon. That day has a name now: Black Monday, the day the Rust Belt was born.

From 1982 to 2000, finance took over. The Dow ran from under one thousand to eleven thousand. Congress repealed Glass-Steagall in 1999 — the law built in 1933 to stop the next 1929.

Nine years later, on September 15, 2008, Lehman Brothers collapsed, the Treasury authorized seven hundred billion in emergency funds, and unemployment hit ten percent.

The cycle repeated.

## CTA / CLOSER

So here's the through-line.

America got rich by inventing machines — financial ones, industrial ones, digital ones. Every machine worked. Every machine broke. And the people who ran them usually needed a depression, a war, or a panic to admit the old machine was done.

The country hit twenty-seven trillion dollars in annual output not because it avoided breaking, but because it rebuilt faster than it fell. Each rebuild moved the bill somewhere else: onto workers, taxpayers, debtors, or the next generation. Growth made the balance sheet larger. It never made the balance sheet disappear.

Whether you think the current machine — whatever it is — will follow the same pattern is up to you. The next break will have winners, losers, and an invoice. But the ledger keeps score either way. Every number leaves a receipt. Someone always pays the balance.
