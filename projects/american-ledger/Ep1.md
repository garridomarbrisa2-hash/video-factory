## Topic research

From a string of tobacco farms clinging to the Atlantic coast to the largest
economy in human history — the through-line is machines and money: every era
of American prosperity was built on one transformative system that eventually
broke.

- **Colonial mercantilism (1612–1776):** In **1612**, **John Rolfe** planted
  Caribbean tobacco seed at **Jamestown** — the first commercial crop. By
  **1617** the colony exported **20,000 pounds** of tobacco to England; twelve
  years later, **1.5 million pounds**. Tobacco became literal currency in the
  Chesapeake — fines, taxes, and wages paid in leaf. England's **Navigation
  Acts** (**1651**, **1660**, **1663**) locked colonial trade into British
  ships and British ports — the empire's mercantilist cage, and a seed of
  revolution.

- **Revolutionary finance (1775–1789):** Congress printed the **Continental
  dollar** to fund the war; over-issue collapsed it into the phrase **"not
  worth a Continental."** The young nation emerged owing roughly **$75–80
  million** — a crushing sum for a country of farmers with no credit.

- **Hamilton's financial system (1790–1795):** Treasury Secretary **Alexander
  Hamilton** delivered the **Report on Public Credit** (**January 9, 1790**),
  pushed through federal **assumption of state debts** (about **$25 million**),
  and won the **Compromise of 1790** — the permanent capital moved to the
  Potomac. The **First Bank of the United States** was chartered **February
  25, 1791**, with **$10 million** in capital — larger than every other
  American corporation combined. The **Report on Manufactures** followed
  (**December 5, 1791**), and the **Whiskey Tax** of **1791** triggered the
  **Whiskey Rebellion** of **1794** — the first test of federal taxing power.

- **Cotton, canals, and early industry (1793–1860):** **Eli Whitney's cotton
  gin** (**1793**) let one worker clean **50 pounds** of lint a day instead of
  5. US cotton output exploded from under **9,000 bales** in **1790** to over
  **4 million bales** by **1860** — roughly **two-thirds of world supply** and
  about **60% of all American exports**. The enslaved population that picked
  it grew from **~700,000** (1790) to **4 million** (1860). Meanwhile the
  **Erie Canal** (**1817–1825**, **363 miles**, **84 locks**, **~$7 million**)
  cut freight costs between the Midwest and New York by over **90%** and made
  New York City the nation's commercial capital. **Samuel Slater's** mill
  (**1790, Pawtucket**) and the **Lowell mills** launched factory industry.

- **Railroads and the robber barons (1862–1907):** The **Homestead Act** and
  **Pacific Railway Act** (both **1862**) gave away land and financed a
  continent-spanning line. The **golden spike** at **Promontory Summit,
  Utah** (**May 10, 1869**) cut New York–San Francisco travel from months to
  **7 days**. Track mileage grew from **~30,000 miles** (1860) to over
  **200,000** (1900). **Cornelius Vanderbilt** consolidated railroads;
  **John D. Rockefeller's Standard Oil** (founded **1870**) controlled **~90%
  of US oil refining**; **Andrew Carnegie** dominated steel — and in **1901**
  sold out to **J.P. Morgan** for **$480 million**, creating **U.S. Steel**,
  the world's first **billion-dollar corporation**. Backlash followed:
  **Interstate Commerce Act** (**1887**), **Sherman Antitrust Act** (**1890**),
  and panics in **1873** and **1893**.

- **The Fed and the creditor nation (1907–1918):** The **Panic of 1907**
  forced the government to rely on **J.P. Morgan** to bail out the banking
  system from his own library. The answer was the **Federal Reserve Act**,
  signed by **Woodrow Wilson** on **December 23, 1913** — **12 regional
  banks** under a Washington board. Then **World War I** flipped America's
  position on the world ledger: the US lent the Allies roughly **$10
  billion**, going from a debtor nation to the world's **creditor** — and
  overtaking Britain as the **largest economy on earth**.

- **Boom and bust (1919–1933):** **Henry Ford's** moving assembly line
  (**1913**) cut Model T build time from **12½ hours to 93 minutes** and the
  price from **$850** (1908) to **$260** (1924) — **15 million** sold. The
  **Dow Jones** climbed to **381.17** on **September 3, 1929**. Then it broke:
  **Black Thursday** (**October 24**), **Black Monday**, **Black Tuesday**
  (**October 29, 1929**). The Dow bottomed at **41.22** on **July 8, 1932** —
  down **89%**. Unemployment hit **25%** by **1933**; national income shrank
  from **$87.8 billion to $40.2 billion**; **9,000 of 25,000 banks** failed.

- **New Deal (1933–1939):** **Franklin D. Roosevelt** closed every bank in
  America in a four-day **bank holiday** (**March 1933**). Then came
  **Glass-Steagall** (**1933**), the **FDIC**, the **SEC** (**1934**),
  **Executive Order 6102** forcing citizens to surrender gold, the **CCC**,
  **WPA**, **TVA** — and the **Social Security Act** (**1935**), the
  foundation of the American safety net.

- **Arsenal of democracy and Bretton Woods (1940–1945):** War production
  doubled US GDP between **1940 and 1945** and drove unemployment to **~1%**.
  American factories turned out nearly **300,000 aircraft**; **~19 million
  women** entered the workforce; national debt climbed to **$258 billion**
  (**~120% of GDP**). In **July 1944**, **44 nations** met at **Bretton
  Woods, New Hampshire** and rebuilt world money around the dollar — pegged
  to gold at **$35 an ounce** — creating the **IMF** and the **World Bank**.

- **The golden age and the Nixon Shock (1945–1973):** In **1945** the US
  produced close to **half the world's output**. The **GI Bill** (**1944**)
  sent millions to college; the **Federal-Aid Highway Act** (**June 29,
  1956**) authorized **$25 billion** for **41,000 miles** of interstate — the
  largest public works project in American history. **Levittown**
  suburbanized the middle class; union membership peaked near **35%**; the
  top marginal tax rate sat at **91%**. Then, on **August 15, 1971**, **Richard
  Nixon** **closed the gold window** — the **Nixon Shock** ended dollar
  convertibility and, by **1973**, the fixed-exchange world.

- **Stagflation and the Rust Belt (1973–1985):** The **1973 OPEC oil
  embargo** quadrupled crude from **~$3 to ~$12 a barrel**; inflation peaked
  near **13.5%** (**1980**); Fed chair **Paul Volcker** drove the federal
  funds rate to **20%** (**June 1981**). Industry hollowed out: on **September
  19, 1977** — **"Black Monday"** — **Youngstown Sheet & Tube's Campbell
  Works** closed, costing **5,000 jobs** in a day and **50,000** across the
  Mahoning Valley within five years. The **Rust Belt** was born.

- **Finance and tech (1980–2008):** Deregulation and the **401(k)** (created
  **1978**) turned America into a nation of shareholders; the **Dow** ran
  from under **1,000** (**1982**) past **10,000** (**1999**).
  **Gramm-Leach-Bliley** (**1999**) repealed Glass-Steagall. **Microsoft**
  (**1975**) and **Apple** (**1976**) seeded the PC era; the **Netscape IPO**
  (**August 1995**) ignited the dot-com boom; the **Nasdaq** peaked at
  **5,048** (**March 10, 2000**), then collapsed to **~1,100**. In **2008**,
  **Lehman Brothers** fell (**September 15**), **TARP** authorized **$700
  billion**, and unemployment hit **10%** — the Great Recession, the worst
  break since 1929.

## Angle / hook

In 1790 the United States was a debtor republic of four million farmers that
couldn't pay its soldiers. A century and a half later it produced half the
world's output. This is the story of how that happened — told through the
machines that built each era: the gin, the canal, the railroad, the assembly
line, the microchip. And the pattern no one escapes: every machine that made
America rich eventually broke, and the country had to invent the next one.
